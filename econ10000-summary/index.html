1

The economic approach

Lecture 1, Sep 29

  • Economics means limited resources and unlimited wants. Becker's approach has four parts: maximizing behavior, equilibrium, stable preferences, and scarcity.
  • Equilibrium means no one has an incentive to change their behavior. It doesn't have to mean supply = demand, and it isn't always good: empty COVID shelves, ~4% unemployment because people quit to find better jobs, and merging early in traffic (nice, but inefficient).
  • Nothing is truly free. Social media costs your time and data, and an “11% rebate” costs the hassle of mailing in receipts.
  • Opportunity cost = explicit + implicit cost. $500/month on vices for 40 years is $240K explicit, but about $3.16M if you had invested it at 10% (Excel: =FV(0.10/12,480,500)). He stressed this one: it's both parts together, not just the hidden cost.
  • Social Security takes 12.4% of pay (6.2% from you, 6.2% from your employer). Invested instead, that could grow to millions, but the program also works as insurance (OASDI).
  • Sunk costs are unrecoverable, so ignore them: “one more game,” a bad movie you already paid for, the F-35's past R&D.
  • Marginal analysis: net benefit peaks where the slopes of total benefit and total cost are equal. That's MB = MC, not the point where TB = TC.
  • Positive economics asks “what is”; normative asks “what ought to be.” He says the line blurs in practice.
    • COVID vaccine paper: fairer distribution would save 670,000 more lives but cost $1.8T (efficiency vs. equity).
    • Framing the same tradeoff two ways changed how the class voted (framing bias).
  • Correlation ≠ causation. Ice cream sales and shark attacks are both driven by summer; the same trap shows up in billionaire dropouts and the WWII bomber picture.
  • Selection bias: grad students may out-earn others because they were stronger to begin with.
  • Difference-in-differences: the grad-school group gained $90K, a similar non-grad group gained $30K anyway, so the true effect is about $60K.
  • Experiments: RCTs, Thaler (2017 Nobel, behavioral economics), and John List's tax-letter field experiment. Check internal validity (was assignment random?) and external validity (does the result apply beyond this sample?).

Check yourself

Where is net benefit largest, and what's true about the slopes of total benefit and total cost there?

Show answer

Where the two curves are farthest apart. Their slopes are equal there: MB = MC.

The grad-school group's earnings rose $90K, and a similar group without grad school rose $30K. What's the effect of grad school?

Show answer

About $60K. Subtract the change the control group had anyway (difference-in-differences).

2

Budget constraint, plus the start of preferences

Lecture 2, Oct 1

  • Goods are sorted by whether they're rival and excludable:
    • Private: bottled water.
    • Club: an uncongested toll road, campus Wi-Fi.
    • Commons: fish in a lake, which get overfished (the volunteer demo).
    • Public: a lighthouse. Free riders mean these are underprovided, so taxes fund them.
    • Goods can switch boxes; congestion adds rivalry.
  • Property rights let people protect a common resource, which makes sustainable use possible. The 2024 Nobel compared Nogales, Arizona with Nogales, Mexico: institutions explain the income gap.
  • Scarcity comes from budget, time, and resources (plus habits and social pressure). The most fundamental limit is 24 hours a day.
  • Budget line: PxX + PyY = I. With apples at $10, oranges at $2, and $50 income, you can buy at most 5 apples or 25 oranges, so y = 25 − 5x.
  • Slope = −Px/Py: the relative price, or the opportunity cost of X in units of Y. Here, 1 apple “costs” 5 oranges.
  • How the line moves:
    • Px rises → the line pivots in and gets steeper. X is relatively pricier and you're poorer, which sets up Lecture 4. His rule for the class: a steeper line means X is relatively more expensive.
    • Income changes → parallel shift.
    • All prices and income rise 20% → the line doesn't move.
  • SNAP example ($5 goods, $50 income):
    • $50 in cash shifts the line out in parallel.
    • $50 for “healthy food only” creates a kinked line. That only matters if you'd have wanted more candy.
    • Same logic as unwanted gifts → deadweight loss (coming later).
  • Other constraint shapes:
    • Work vs. leisure: the slope is the real wage w/p, the opportunity cost of an hour of rest.
    • Risk vs. return (CAPM): upward-sloping, because risk is a “bad.”
    • Cleaning vs. studying: bowed out, meaning increasing opportunity cost.
    • Spend $240K now vs. have $3.2M at retirement.
  • Preferences are assumed stable. His 20-lb weight loss came from learning sugar's true “price,” not from new tastes.
  • Rational preferences are complete (you can rank any two bundles) and transitive (A > B and B > C → A > C). Extra assumptions: more is better, balanced beats extreme, and continuity.
  • Utility is just a number that ranks bundles. It is not happiness.
  • An indifference curve is a set of bundles with equal utility. For example, 70 food + 10 housing and 50 food + 20 housing both give 516 utils. You'd give up 20 food for your first extra 10 housing but far less later, so the curve bows inward (diminishing marginal utility).

Check yourself

Apples $10, oranges $2, income $50. What does the slope of −5 tell you?

Show answer

One apple costs you 5 oranges. It's the relative price, which is the opportunity cost of an apple.

How does the budget line move when income rises, and when Px rises?

Show answer

Income up: a parallel shift out. Px up: it pivots in around the Y-axis intercept and gets steeper.

3

Indifference curves and utility maximization

Lecture 3, Oct 6

  • Think of utility as a “mountain”; indifference curves are its contour lines. Farther out = higher utility.
  • One person's ICs can't cross, because that violates transitivity. That's why answering “C” to both clicker questions was a problem.
  • ICs also can't be thick (that violates “more is better”). With two goods they slope down and bow in. If one item is a “bad,” like risk, the IC slopes up.
  • Slope of IC = MRS = MUx/MUy: how many Y you'd give up for one more X.
    • Derivation: steal some Y, then give back just enough X to make you whole, so loss + gain = 0.
    • Keep x on top. He called flipping it a common exam mistake.
  • Where you have lots of Y and little X, MUx is high and MUy is low, so the IC is steep. With an all-vanilla sundae, you'd trade 3 vanilla scoops for 1 chocolate. Unlike the budget line, the IC's slope changes along the curve.
  • Extras: the water–diamond paradox (scarce diamonds have high marginal utility), perfect substitutes (Coke/Pepsi: straight line), and perfect complements (left/right socks: L-shape).
  • Utility tables ($7, both goods $1):
    • Chasing the biggest totals (5X and 2Y) gives 295 utils.
    • Spending each dollar where marginal utility is highest (3X and 4Y) gives 330 utils.
    • Multiplying every utility by 10 changes nothing, since only rankings matter.
  • With Px = $2 and Py = $1, compare marginal utility per dollar. The answer is 2X and 3Y (280 utils), and the last dollar on each good gives 30 utils.
  • The rule: MUx/Px = MUy/Py (equal bang for the buck). Rearranged, it says MUx/MUy = Px/Py, so the IC is tangent to the budget line. He called this the most important formula in consumer theory.
  • Intuition: if your willingness to pay (MRS) beats the market's price ratio, “take the deal” and buy more X until they match. If the IC crosses the budget line, you can still do better.

Check yourself

Why can't one person's indifference curves cross?

Show answer

It would break transitivity: a bundle with more of both goods would end up “equal” to one with less.

Px = $2 and Py = $1. The next unit of X adds 30 utils and the next unit of Y adds 20. Which do you buy?

Show answer

Y. It gives 20 utils per dollar, versus 30 ÷ $2 = 15 for X.

4

Demand curve and price effects

Lecture 4, Oct 8

  • Pop quiz setup: Px = $20, Py = $10, and you'd give 6Y for 1X. He hinted this could be a midterm question.
    • MRS (6) > Px/Py (2), so you're not at the optimum.
    • X gives more bang per buck, so buy more X and less Y.
  • Equation method, with 10X + 5Y = 45 and MRS = X/2Y:
    • Step 1: set X/2Y = 2, so X = 4Y.
    • Step 2: plug into the budget line → Y* = 1, X* = 4.
  • Deriving demand: at Px = $20 the best choice is 4 units. At Px = $5 the line rotates out and the best choice is 10 units. Plot (4, $20) and (10, $5), connect them, and that's the demand curve.
  • Every point on the demand curve is a utility-maximizing choice, and its height is your willingness to pay. At 4 units, the MRS is 4Y, and 4Y × $5 = $20. The curve slopes down because of diminishing marginal utility.
  • Consumer surplus = willingness to pay − price, summed over units. Graphically, it's the triangle under demand and above price.
    • Example: P = 30 − 2x at P = $10 → 10 units, so CS = ½ × 10 × 20 = $100.
    • The first unit alone was worth $28 to you, and you paid $10.
  • Market demand = add quantities horizontally. Two identical people give P = 30 − X: same starting price, flatter line. He said about half of students get this wrong on the midterm.
  • Price increase (eggflation, cigarette tax): moving from A (10 units) to B (4 units) is a total effect of −6, and you end up on a lower IC. To split it, slide the new budget line out parallel until it touches the old IC at point C (6 units). This is the “hypothetical budget line.”
    • Substitution effect, A → C (−4): X is relatively pricier, so you swap toward Y. It always goes opposite the price change.
    • Income effect, C → B (−2): your real buying power fell, so you buy less of a normal good.
  • For an inferior good, the income effect flips: poorer → buy more X. That's why he questioned whether cigarette taxes deter smoking. Some smokers may cut healthy food instead.

Check yourself

Two identical people each have demand P = 30 − 2x. What's the market demand curve?

Show answer

P = 30 − X. At each price, double the quantity: same intercept, flatter line.

Px rises. Which effect always pushes you to buy less X, and which one can flip?

Show answer

The substitution effect always means less X. The income effect flips for an inferior good.
